## Teaching standard

1. State one observable outcome in language a child can understand. Distinguish the skill practised here from the full syllabus objective.
2. Check a necessary earlier skill before introducing the new one. A child who struggles gets a relevant picture and an explanation.
3. Introduce one concrete question. Keep its quantities, colours, units and object meanings consistent until the explanation is complete.
4. Ask a prediction that gives the following action a purpose. The interaction must expose the mathematical relationship, with undo or reset where appropriate.
5. Explain what changed, what stayed the same, and why. Every annotation must identify something visible. A caption must not silently reveal an answer being assessed.
6. Announce a new example before changing values. Let the child contribute a step before revealing its answer; reduce support across practice.
7. Make each assessment form require a different action. In a visual question the picture carries necessary information; it is not counted as optional help.
8. Explain likely wrong answers specifically. Offer one smaller question at a time, return to the original question, and record help honestly.
9. End by reconnecting the result with the relationship, then suggest a short delayed review.
10. Verify correctness, interaction, keyboard operation and narrow layouts. These checks cannot substitute for observing whether a child understands.

## Teacher and pupil review

Ask a teacher to check the outcome, prerequisites, vocabulary, examples, misconceptions and scope. Then observe a child in the intended grade using the lesson without navigation coaching. Record the exact screen and wording whenever the child asks “why are we doing this?” or loses track of a quantity.

At three points ask the child to explain:

- Before acting: “What are you trying to find, and what do you think will change?”
- After acting: “Show me what each part of this picture means. Why does this calculation match it?”
- After practice: “Can you solve a new example and explain why a tempting wrong answer is wrong?”

Record whether the child answered independently, needed a prompt, or needed teaching. Review the same relationship later with a different example. Do not treat stars, completion, or a correct multiple-choice answer as evidence of lasting understanding.

## Remaining depth work

1. Review representative lessons from each family with teachers and children. Record whether the child can explain the relationship without navigation coaching.
2. Deepen the current construction tasks into investigations with more than one valid arrangement where mathematically appropriate. Current fraction-strip construction selects a prefix of pieces; it is not free rearrangement.
3. Broaden triangle examples to oblique triangles and internal/external perpendicular heights. The three-lesson triangle reference still uses matching right triangles.
4. Pupil-generated explanations and adaptive diagnosis. Since 18 September, generated questions carry situation-specific misconceptions (83% of items), easier recovery questions of the same kind (73%), worked alternative methods and error-analysis reasoning items; see QA.md. The misconceptions are drawn from general knowledge of common errors rather than classroom data, nothing yet branches on a pupil's pattern of mistakes, and pupils still choose explanations rather than write their own.
5. Add delayed transfer tasks across representations and contexts. The five-to-seven-item in-app check is evidence about those questions, not validated long-term mastery.

A universal framework helps prevent flow breaks, but does not replace reviewing the mathematical wording and visual meaning of each lesson.
